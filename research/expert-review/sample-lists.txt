--- AROUND THE HOUSE --- (One-time tasks and plans. Most are free and take under an hour. ) Still to get: $45–150
  Make a plan
    [ ] Make a household plan {level:basics, plan:weekend}
        why: Pick an out-of-area contact everyone can text, plus two meeting spots: one right outside, and one outside your neighborhood in case you can't get home. | tip: Decide who picks up kids and pets if you can't get home. After a big quake, local calls often fail but texts and long-distance calls can get through. Write the plan on paper, put a copy in every bag, and go over it once a year.
    [ ] Practice Drop, Cover, and Hold On {level:basics, plan:weekend, for: earthquake}
        why: Drop to your hands and knees, cover your head and neck under a sturdy table, and hold on until the shaking stops. Don't run outside. | tip: In bed? Stay there and cover your head with a pillow. In a wheelchair? Lock the wheels and cover your head and neck. Practice once with everyone at home. The Great ShakeOut drill is the third Thursday in October, a good day to invite the neighbors.
    [ ] Learn your building's emergency plan and stairwells {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Elevators stop after quakes and in outages. Keep your go bag by the door and know where you'd meet outside. | tip: If anyone can't do stairs, work out a plan with them and a few neighbors ahead of time.
  Neighbors and community
    [ ] Swap numbers with a neighbor {level:basics, plan:weekend}
        why: After a big disaster, most help comes from people nearby. Swap numbers and agree to text each other after a quake, fire, or long outage. | tip: Ask what would help them, and say what would help you, like a spare key, a pet, or a ride. If someone nearby lives alone, ask if they'd like a check-in.
    [ ] Print an OK / HELP sign for your window {level:basics, plan:month, for: earthquake}
        why: After a big quake, a sign in the window tells neighbors and crews who needs help first, so they can skip homes that are fine. | tip: Keep it by the front door with some tape. Put up HELP even if you can't get to the door.
  Sign-ups and paperwork
    [ ] Sign up for your county's emergency alerts {level:basics, plan:weekend}
        why: Free texts and calls about evacuations, fires, and other emergencies near you. Most are opt-in, and few people sign up. | tip: Sign up with your home address, plus work or school if they're in a different county.
    [ ] Photograph your IDs, insurance, and prescriptions {level:basics, plan:weekend}
        why: If you lose your wallet or have to leave fast, a secure copy makes it much easier to get help, refill medicine, and file claims. | tip: Save them somewhere password-protected that you can reach from any device.
    [ ] Sign up for PG&E outage alerts for your address {level:basics, plan:month, for: outage}
        why: PG&E warns people before planned power shutoffs, usually a day or two ahead. You don't need a PG&E account, so renters can sign up too. | tip: You can add more than one address, like work, school, or a parent's place.
    [ ] Review your insurance {level:basics, plan:month, for: earthquake}
        why: Standard home and renters policies don't cover earthquake or flood damage. California Earthquake Authority renters policies start around $35 a year and help pay for a place to stay. | tip: Renters: your landlord's policy doesn't cover your things. Check if you're in a flood zone at msc.fema.gov. Flood insurance usually takes 30 days to start, so buy it before the rainy season.
    [ ] Set up your phone for emergencies {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Turn on emergency alerts in Settings, add Emergency SOS contacts and a Medical ID, download an offline map of the Bay Area, and install MyShake for earthquake warnings. | tip: Each takes a couple of minutes. Android phones have quake alerts built in, but MyShake is a good backup. Newer iPhones and some Android phones can text 911 by satellite with no signal. Try the demo in Settings.
  Make your home safer
    [ ] Keep shoes, a flashlight, and a whistle by every bed {level:basics, plan:weekend, for: earthquake}
        why: Night-time quakes break glass, and the power goes out instantly. A whistle helps rescuers find you if you're stuck. | tip: Put them in a small bag tied to the bed leg so it doesn't slide away. Add spare glasses if you wear them.
    [ ] Find your gas, water, and power shutoffs {level:basics, plan:month, for: earthquake}
        why: Only turn off the gas if you smell gas or hear hissing. Once it's off, only PG&E can turn it back on, which can take days. | tip: In a house, keep a wrench by the gas meter, about $15. In an apartment, ask your building manager where these are and who handles them. If pipes break after a quake, close the water main to keep the clean water in your pipes and water heater.
    [ ] Test smoke alarms and add a carbon monoxide alarm — $0–40 {level:basics, plan:month}

    [ ] Fire extinguisher — $25–50 {level:basics, plan:month}

  Smoke and air

        why: On smoky days, close windows and doors, run a filter in one room, and spend most of your time there, especially to sleep. | tip: Choose a room with few windows. Check the air with the AirNow app and close up before the smoke arrives. Set your AC or car vents to recirculate.

--- STAY-AT-HOME BIN --- (Think of it as camping at home: no power, no running water, stores closed, and help may be slow to reach you. Keep it somewhere you can reach after a quake: a closet near the door, not the back of the garage.) Still to get: $240–490
  Something to keep it in
    [ ] Large plastic bin with a lid — 1 bin — $15–30 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Keeps everything together and dry, in one place you can find in the dark. | tip: Wheels help if it gets heavy. Water can sit next to it.
  Water
    [x] Drinking water — 7 gallons (That's about 2 jugs of 5 gallons, or 3 cases of 24 bottles. If that's a lot, start with 3 gallons (3 days) and add more over time.) — $7–14 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Plan on 1 gallon per person per day, for drinking and basic cleaning. After a big quake, tap water may be off or unsafe for days or weeks. | tip: Kids, nursing parents, sick people, and anyone in a heat wave need more. Store it somewhere cool and dark, away from chemicals. Replace store-bought water by its date, and water you bottled from the tap every 6 months. Pets are counted in the amount.
    [ ] Unscented bleach — 1 bottle — $3–6 {level:basics, plan:month, for: earthquake}
        why: To make questionable water safe to drink. EBMUD's dose: 8 drops of 6% bleach (or 6 drops of 8.25%) per gallon of clear water, then wait 30 minutes. Boiling for 1 minute also works. | tip: If the water is cloudy, let it settle and pour it through a clean cloth first, then use twice as much bleach. Use plain bleach with no scent or "splash-less" formula, and replace it every year.
  Food
    [ ] Canned protein: beans, tuna, chicken, or chili — 7 cans — $11–21 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: The base of your food plan: about one can per person per day. Together with the other food rows, that's 1,200 to 1,500 calories a day each. Ready.gov says healthy people can get by on that for a while, so you don't need full meals. | tip: Most kitchens already have a few days of food. Buy what you already eat, so you can rotate it into meals and replace it. Pull-tab lids save needing the opener.
    [ ] Canned fruit and vegetables — 7 cans — $7–18 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: About one can per person per day, for vitamins and fiber. The juice counts toward what you drink.
    [ ] Crackers, cereal, or granola — 2 boxes — $6–10 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: About two servings per person per day. A box has around eight. | tip: Pick sealed boxes or sleeves. They go stale fast once opened.
    [ ] Peanut butter or other nut butter — 1 jar — $3–6 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: A spoonful or two each a day. Lots of calories and protein, and no cooking. A jar has about 14 servings. | tip: Sunflower seed butter works for nut allergies.
    [ ] Granola bars, trail mix, or dried fruit — 7 snacks — $5–11 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: One a day each, for energy between meals. A familiar treat helps a lot when things are stressful, especially for kids.
    [ ] Manual can opener — 1 opener — $5–12 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Electric openers won't work without power.
  First aid and medicine
    [ ] First aid kit — 1 kit — $25–50 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Cuts, scrapes, and sprains are the most common injuries after a quake. Help may be slow to arrive. | tip: Make sure it has bandages, gauze, tape, alcohol wipes, nitrile gloves, and tweezers. A small kit is fine for the go bag. A printed guide or the Red Cross First Aid app works without internet.
    [ ] Everyday medicines — $15–30 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Pain reliever, allergy medicine, anti-diarrhea medicine, and a thermometer. Pharmacies may be closed.
  Toilet and hygiene
    [ ] Toilet paper — $5–12 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Stores may run out, and you'll go through more than you think.
    [ ] Hand soap — $3–8 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Sanitizer doesn't work well on dirty hands. A little soap and water does.
    [ ] Hand sanitizer — $3–8 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Keeping hands clean prevents illness when there's no running water. | tip: Look for at least 60% alcohol.
    [ ] Wet wipes — $4–10 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: For washing up when there's no water to spare.
    [ ] Menstrual products — $5–12 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: A cycle's worth or more for anyone who uses them. Easy to forget and hard to find after a disaster. | tip: Skip this if no one in the house needs them.
    [ ] Bucket with a lid, for an emergency toilet — 1 bucket — $8–15 {level:basics, plan:month, for: earthquake}
        why: Don't flush after a big quake until you know the sewer lines are OK. A 5-gallon bucket lined with heavy bags, plus kitty litter or sawdust, works. | tip: Snap-on toilet seats for buckets are cheap. A second bucket to keep pee and poop separate cuts the smell a lot.
    [ ] Heavy-duty trash bags — 30+ bags — $10–20 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: To line the toilet bucket, and for trash, since garbage pickup may stop. Get more than you think.
    [ ] Kitty litter or sawdust — $5–12 {level:basics, plan:month, for: earthquake}
        why: A scoop after each use in the toilet bucket keeps the smell down.
  Light and power
    [x] Flashlight or headlamp — 1 light — $8–15 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: One for each person. Headlamps leave your hands free. | tip: Skip candles. They're a leading cause of fires after earthquakes.
    [ ] Extra batteries — $10–25 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Enough to replace every battery in your lights and radio at least once. | tip: Check sizes before you buy. Store them out of the devices so they don't leak.
    [ ] Phone power bank — 1 power bank — $15–35 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Your phone is your flashlight, radio, and lifeline. A charged power bank keeps it going for a few days. | tip: 10,000 mAh charges most phones two or three times. Recharge it every few months.
  Alerts and staying in touch
    [ ] Battery or hand-crank radio — 1 radio — $25–45 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: If cell networks are down, local radio is how officials share news. KCBS 740 AM and KQED 88.5 FM cover the Bay Area, and NOAA Weather Radio is on 162.400 MHz. | tip: Look for one with NOAA weather bands and a USB port to charge a phone.
    [ ] Important phone numbers on paper {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: If your phone dies, do you know anyone's number? Include your out-of-area contact, doctors, and insurance. | tip: Kids carry one too, with your numbers, in their bag or pocket. Help them memorize one number.
  Smoke and air
    [ ] N95 masks — 5 masks — $5–8 {level:basics, plan:month, for: smoke,earthquake}
        why: They filter out the fine particles in wildfire smoke, plus dust after a quake. Cloth and surgical masks don't. | tip: Look for "NIOSH approved" on the box. They need a snug fit, so pick a size for each person. N95s aren't made for young kids, so keep them indoors with clean air instead. Extras are easy to share with neighbors on smoky days.
    [ ] Box fan with a filter taped on — $40–80 {level:basics, plan:month, for: smoke}
        why: A cheap, proven air cleaner for your clean-air room: a box fan with a furnace filter taped to the back. Look for MERV 13 on the package. | tip: Four filters taped into a cube (a Corsi-Rosenthal box) works even better. Smoke clogs filters fast, so keep a spare and swap it when it turns dark gray.
  Clothing and warmth
    [ ] Blankets or sleeping bags — 1 blanket {level:basics, plan:month, for: earthquake,outage}
        why: Without heat, Bay Area nights get cold fast.
  Tools
    [ ] Sturdy work gloves — 1 pair — $6–12 {level:basics, plan:month, for: earthquake}
        why: For clearing broken glass and debris without cutting your hands.
  Documents and cash
    [ ] Cash in small bills — $100 or more {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Card readers and ATMs don't work without power or internet. | tip: Keep some ones and fives. Stores may not have change.

--- "WHEN IT HAPPENS" CARD ---
  Right after a big quake
    1. Drop, cover, and hold on until the shaking stops. In bed, stay there and cover your head with a pillow. Expect aftershocks.
    2. Put on shoes. Check yourself, then the people around you. Press hard on any bleeding.
    3. Smell gas or hear hissing? Get everyone out, shut off the gas if you can, and call PG&E from outside. Otherwise leave the gas on.
    4. Use flashlights, not candles. Never run a generator, grill, or camp stove indoors.
    5. If the building looks badly damaged, take your go bags and leave. Leave a note saying where you went.
    6. Text, don't call. Send one "We're OK" to your out-of-area contact, then save your battery.
    7. Don't flush until you know the sewer is OK. Use the bucket toilet. If pipes broke, close the water main.
    8. Put your OK or HELP sign in the window, and check in with your neighbors.
    9. Listen to a battery radio (KCBS 740 AM or KQED 88.5 FM) and your county alerts. For shelter or help, call 211.
  Other emergencies
    1. Power out: keep the fridge and freezer shut. A full freezer stays cold about 2 days.

--- "WHEN IT HAPPENS" CARD ---
  Right after a big quake
    1. Drop, cover, and hold on until the shaking stops. In bed, stay there and cover your head with a pillow. Expect aftershocks.
    2. Put on shoes. Check yourself, then the people around you. Press hard on any bleeding.
    3. Smell gas or hear hissing? Get everyone out, shut off the gas if you can, and call PG&E from outside. Otherwise leave the gas on.
    4. Use flashlights, not candles. Never run a generator, grill, or camp stove indoors.
    5. If the building looks badly damaged, take your go bags and leave. Leave a note saying where you went.
    6. Text, don't call. Send one "We're OK" to your out-of-area contact, then save your battery.
    7. Don't flush until you know the sewer is OK. Use the bucket toilet. If pipes broke, close the water main.
    8. Put your OK or HELP sign in the window, and check in with your neighbors.
    9. Listen to a battery radio (KCBS 740 AM or KQED 88.5 FM) and your county alerts. For shelter or help, call 211.
  Other emergencies
    1. Fire near you: if you feel unsafe, leave. Don't wait for an evacuation order.
    2. Power out: keep the fridge and freezer shut. A full freezer stays cold about 2 days.

  Make your home safer
    [ ] Keep shoes, a flashlight, and a whistle by every bed {level:basics, plan:weekend, for: earthquake}

    [ ] Find your gas, water, and power shutoffs {level:basics, plan:month, for: earthquake,flood}

    [ ] Test smoke alarms and add a carbon monoxide alarm — $0–40 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: Fires are common after quakes and during outages. Generators, grills, and gas heaters used during outages cause carbon monoxide poisoning. | tip: Press the test button monthly.     [ ] If you have an electric garage door, learn to open it by hand {level:basics, plan:month, for: outage,wildfire,earthquake}

        why: Falling bookcases, TVs, and heavy objects over beds and couches cause many quake injuries at home, especially to kids. | tip: Latch kitchen cabinets and put museum putty under breakables. Renters can usually use straps with small screw holes. Ask your landlord first.
    [ ] Fire extinguisher — $25–50 {level:basics, plan:month}
        why: A small fire can be put out before it spreads. Keep it in the kitchen and learn how to use it: pull, aim, squeeze, sweep. | tip: Get one rated ABC, at least 2-A:10-B:C, and check the gauge once a year. A fire blanket is a good extra for the kitchen.
    [ ] Make sure your water heater is strapped — $30–60 {level:basics, plan:month, for: earthquake}
        why: California law requires it. A falling water heater can break the gas line and start a fire. If it stays upright, its 30 to 50 gallons can be used for flushing and cleaning. | tip: If you rent, strapping it is your landlord's job. Ask them to check.     [ ] Check if your house needs a seismic retrofit {level:advanced, plan:later, for: earthquake}
        why: Older wood houses can slide off their foundations. Owners can get grants of up to $3,000 from California's Earthquake Brace + Bolt program. If you rent, ask your landlord whether it's been done. | tip: Grant sign-ups open for a few weeks most years. Sign up on the site to be told when.
    [ ] Cover attic and crawlspace vents with fine metal mesh — $30–120 {level:advanced, plan:later, for: wildfire}
        why: Embers getting in through vents are a main way homes catch fire. Metal mesh with 1/8-inch or smaller holes keeps them out. Regular screen and plastic mesh don't. | tip: About $5 to $15 a vent. If you rent, ask your landlord.
  Food

    [ ] Fridge and freezer thermometers — $5–12 {level:advanced, plan:later, for: outage}

        why: For people who rely on powered medical devices. It lowers your rate and adds extra check-ins before planned shutoffs.
    [ ] See if you qualify for a free backup battery {level:basics, plan:month, for: outage}
        why: PG&E's Disability Disaster Access and Resources program can provide portable batteries, accessible transport, and hotel stays during planned shutoffs. Income isn't considered.
    [ ] Line up a few people who'd check on you {level:basics, plan:weekend}

  Smoke and air
    [ ] Pick one room to be your clean-air room {level:basics, plan:month, for: smoke}

--- "WHEN IT HAPPENS" CARD ---
  Right after a big quake
    1. Drop, cover, and hold on until the shaking stops. In bed, stay there and cover your head with a pillow. Expect aftershocks.
    2. Put on shoes. Check yourself, then the people around you. Press hard on any bleeding.
    3. Smell gas or hear hissing? Get everyone out, shut off the gas if you can, and call PG&E from outside. Otherwise leave the gas on.
    4. Use flashlights, not candles. Never run a generator, grill, or camp stove indoors.
    5. If the building looks badly damaged, take your go bags and leave. Leave a note saying where you went.
    6. Near the coast and felt a long or strong quake? Walk to high ground now. Don't wait for an alert.
    7. Text, don't call. Send one "We're OK" to your out-of-area contact, then save your battery.
    8. Don't flush until you know the sewer is OK. Use the bucket toilet. If pipes broke, close the water main.
    9. Put your OK or HELP sign in the window, and check in with your neighbors.
    10. Listen to a battery radio (KCBS 740 AM or KQED 88.5 FM) and your county alerts. For shelter or help, call 211.
  Other emergencies
    1. Fire near you: if you feel unsafe, leave. Don't wait for an evacuation order.
    2. Flooding: turn around, don't drown. Six inches of moving water can knock you down.
    3. Power out: keep the fridge and freezer shut. A full freezer stays cold about 2 days.
